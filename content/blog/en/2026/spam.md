When you hear the word "SPAM," what comes to mind — the tasty "luncheon meat," or that damned "junk email"?

Let me tell you where the word SPAM came from.

## A can of luncheon meat with a surprisingly short ingredient list

Today's [SPAM luncheon meat](https://www.spam.com/what-is-spam-brand) is made of remarkably few things: pork and ham, salt, water, potato starch, sugar, sodium nitrite. That's it. Nothing else. The original 1937 recipe didn't even have the potato starch — that was only added in 2009, to reduce the layer of gelatin that forms after the meat is cooked in the can. So unlike today's ultra-processed foods, its ingredient list is comparatively simple. As for the pork, it's mostly pork shoulder. Back in the 1930s, pork shoulder wasn't considered a desirable cut — it was harder to sell and worth less. Jay Catherwood Hormel spotted the opportunity, seasoned it, and put it on the market.

## What is SPAM actually short for?

The name came out of a brand-naming contest that Hormel Foods held on 5 July 1937. Ken Daigneau — the brother of one of Hormel's vice presidents — won with the name "SPAM®" and took home a $100 prize.

To this day nobody can fully confirm what SPAM is short for. The official FAQ only says that the true meaning of SPAM is known to just a small circle of former Hormel executives. So some people say it's [Shoulder of Pork and Ham](https://blog.trendmicro.com.tw/?p=149), others say it's simply Spiced Ham, and still others insist on "Specially Processed American Meat" or "Specially Processed Army Meat." As for which is true — in the presence of delicious SPAM, that may not matter all that much.

## Why does Chinese call it "lunch meat"?

So why does Chinese call this kind of food 午餐肉, literally "lunch meat"?

It comes from the English "Lunch Meat (Luncheon Meat)," originally a general term for processed meats that were pre-cooked and pressed into shape so they could be sliced and eaten cold or heated up — commonly used in sandwiches, cold platters, or quick meals. Americans at the time often put lunch meat in a sandwich for lunch, hence the name. SPAM is one of the best known brands of this kind of canned luncheon meat.

## Not necessarily cheaper, but convenient enough

When SPAM launched in 1937, a 12-ounce can was [advertised at roughly 28 to 35 cents](https://passionatefoodie.blogspot.com/2023/04/the-origins-of-spam-miracle-meat.html), with 29 cents being one common price. That works out to about 37 to 47 cents a pound — not necessarily cheaper than every fresh meat of the day, and in fact higher than some fresh pork per pound. But SPAM was already cooked, boneless, almost entirely edible, needed no refrigeration, and kept for a long time. In terms of reducing waste, simplifying cooking, and providing a stable supply, it had economic advantages fresh meat didn't always offer.

What really made SPAM spread fast was the large-scale military procurement and global shipping demand of the Second World War. SPAM needed no refrigeration, was easy to crate, kept well, and provided a reliable source of meat protein — all of which made it ideal for shipping overseas. Hormel's official history records that more than 100 million pounds of SPAM luncheon meat were shipped abroad during the war to supply Allied forces. As American and Allied supply networks expanded, [SPAM also reached Britain, the Pacific islands, and much of Asia](https://time.com/5593886/asian-american-spam-cuisine), gradually becoming a globally recognizable wartime food.

## So how did it come to mean "junk email"?

Up to this point, SPAM is still just a canned luncheon meat brand. So why do we now use it to mean "junk email"?

The answer starts with British wartime and postwar food memories — and with one absurd comedy sketch from 1970.

After the Second World War broke out, Britain was heavily dependent on overseas imports, and shipping was under threat from the war, so food rationing began in 1940. Meat, bacon, ham, butter, sugar, and other staples were all restricted. Even after the war ended, shortages and rationing continued for years; meat rationing wasn't fully lifted until 1954. In that environment, canned meats like SPAM — no refrigeration needed, easy to store and transport — were genuinely useful. It also arrived in Britain in large quantities along with Allied supplies, becoming a conspicuous presence in wartime and postwar eating. That said, rather than calling SPAM a "cheap meat substitute" that every British household depended on, it's more accurate to say it became a vivid cultural symbol of wartime canned food, rationed living, and monotonous menus.

On 15 December 1970, BBC1 aired episode 12 of the second series of *Monty Python's Flying Circus*. Its "[Spam](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y9B75hH26yU)" sketch pushed the impression of SPAM being *everywhere* to an absurd extreme: nearly every dish on a small café's menu contains SPAM, while a group of Vikings in the corner keep chanting "[Spam, Spam, Spam](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/cs181/projects/spam/sketch.HTML)." The word SPAM appears [well over a hundred times](https://www.foodnext.net/science/machining/paper/3616168965)!

Inspired by the "Spam" sketch, users on the scrolling text terminals that emerged in the 1980s — BBSes, BITNET Relay, MUDs — started typing "spam" over and over, or dumping the entire Spam Song, text files, and ASCII art into chat rooms so that normal conversation scrolled off the screen. This prank came to be called spamming. The earliest record of it is from 26 September 1990, when Mat Watson asked on rec.games.mud what spamming actually meant — he saw it constantly but wasn't sure of the definition, and guessed it might have to do with entering large numbers of commands, causing server problems, or crashing things.

Then on 31 March 1993, Richard Depew was testing a Usenet auto-moderation program called ARMM (Automated Retroactive Minimal Moderation). A recursion bug caused it to post around 200 messages in a row to a Usenet group, each subject line gaining another "ARMM:" prefix while the headers and Message-IDs grew longer and longer — reportedly breaking at least one mail system in the process. Group member Joel Furr borrowed "spam," a word already popular in the MUD community, to describe the flood. [That incident brought the SPAM usage into Usenet](https://www.templetons.com/brad/spamterm.html), and through the large-scale online advertising incidents that followed, it gradually became the general term for junk messages.

## The first junk email in history wasn't called spam yet

Of course, that wasn't the first junk message in history. The [first junk email ever sent](https://www.templetons.com/brad/spamreact.html) was on 1 May 1978, when Gary Thuerk, a marketer at DEC (Digital Equipment Corporation), used the ARPANET directory to send about 400 accounts an invitation to a product demonstration for the DECSYSTEM-20 (a mainframe computer). This is widely regarded as the earliest fully documented case of bulk commercial junk email. It quickly drew protests from recipients and from ARPANET administrators, because it consumed public research network resources, violated the acceptable use rules, and gave companies with network access an unfair commercial advantage. At this point, though, the word SPAM hadn't yet attached itself to any of this — people still used the conventional terms "electronic junk mail" and "bulk advertising."

Through the word SPAM, we can see how words keep evolving in the great current of history, as though they were alive and endlessly reproducing, turning into shared memory across several generations. It's as if our own memory is being spammed continuously — and by the look of things, it will keep spamming for a good while yet.
